Retrieval, no notes: A2.2.4 segmentation and earlier

Four quick ones

Why does segmenting a network reduce congestion?

Each segment is a smaller broadcast domain, so a broadcast stays in its segment and each device receives less traffic.

Friday: 10.20.30.15 and 10.20.40.15. Same subnet?

No: different network part (10.20.30 vs 10.20.40).

Which server gives a laptop its IP address when it joins?

The DHCP server (often built into the router).

Switch or router: which uses IP addresses to forward between networks?

The router. A switch forwards within one LAN using MAC addresses.

A2.3.1 IP addressing: IPv4 and IPv6

Two address formats

IPv4: 32 bits

192.168.1.10

Four numbers, each 0 to 255 (8 bits), separated by dots: dotted decimal.

About 4.3 billion addresses.

IPv6: 128 bits

2001:0db8:85a3:0000:0000:8a2e:0370:7334

Eight groups of hexadecimal digits (0 to 9, a to f), separated by colons.

About 3.4 × 1038 addresses.

Why was IPv6 introduced?

IPv4 ran out of addresses once phones, laptops and smart devices came online. Not for speed, not for security.

About how many people are there per IPv4 address?

About 8 billion people, 4.3 billion addresses: fewer than one address each, before counting a single phone or laptop.

A2.3.1 IP addressing: public and private, static and dynamic

Where an address works, and who sets it

Public

Unique across the whole internet, assigned by the ISP, reachable from anywhere. Your home router has one.

Private

Used only inside a local network, not routable on the internet, so the same one is reused in millions of homes. Ranges: 10.x.x.x, 172.16.x.x to 172.31.x.x, 192.168.x.x.

Static

Set by hand, never changes. For devices others must always find: web and mail servers, network printers.

Dynamic

Lent by DHCP from a pool, may change next time. For everyday devices: phones, laptops, consoles.

"NAT is a security feature." True?

No. Its job is addressing: it minimizes the public addresses needed and lets private devices reach the internet. Outsiders seeing only the public address is a side effect, not a firewall.

A2.3.3 Packet switching: what is in a packet

A payload wrapped in a header

Source address81.2.69.160
Destination address151.101.0.81
Sequence number3 of 4
Error checka value worked out from the data
Payloadone piece of the message

The message is segmented into packets. Each header field is there because of something that can happen on the way.

Why a destination address in every packet?

So each packet can be routed on its own.

Why a source address?

So the receiver knows where it came from, and where to send replies or requests for resends.

Why a sequence number?

Because packets can arrive out of order: the destination uses it to put them back in order.

Why an error check?

Because a packet can be corrupted on the way: the destination detects it and asks for that packet again.

Switch and router, two different jobs

In the simulator, which device decided that packet 2 took a different route from packet 1?

A router: it makes the routing decision for each packet separately.

"A switch routes packets across the internet." What is wrong?

A switch only works inside one LAN, by MAC address. Routing between networks is the router's job.

A2.3.2 Transmission media: how each one carries data

Light, electricity, radio

Fiber optic

Pulses of light in a thin glass or plastic core. The cladding around it reflects the light back in, so it stays in the core.

Twisted pair

Electrical signals on pairs of copper wires. The twisting cancels out much of the interference. Most Ethernet cabling.

Wireless

Radio waves through the air. No cable, so devices can move, but the air is shared with everything nearby.

Attenuation

The signal weakening as it travels along the medium.

Interference

An outside signal disrupting it, such as motors, other cables or other Wi-Fi.

Not the same thing: a cable can have low attenuation and still pick up interference.

A2.3.2 Transmission media: choose the medium, in pairs

Which medium, and which factor decided it?

Linking two school buildings 2 km apart

Fiber optic: range (twisted pair stops at about 100 m) and bandwidth for everything that crosses between them.

Thirty desktop PCs in one computer lab

Twisted pair: cheap and simple over short runs, steady and reliable.

Visitors' phones in the school theater

Wireless: mobility, no cabling; encrypt it, and keep it on its own segment (Friday).

A factory floor beside heavy electric motors

Fiber optic: interference. Light is not affected by electromagnetic interference; copper and radio are.

Exam technique, 4 minutes: compare factor by factor

Compare twisted pair and wireless with reference to reliability and security. [4]

Answer A

"Twisted pair is a copper cable used in offices. It is reliable and fairly secure. Wireless uses radio waves and lets people move around. It can be hacked."

1 mark at most: each medium described on its own; no sentence sets them against each other.

Answer B

"Reliability: twisted pair gives a steadier connection because the signal stays inside the cable, while wireless varies with distance, obstacles and interference, so twisted pair is the more reliable. Security: an attacker must physically reach a twisted pair cable, while wireless broadcasts into the air where anyone in range can intercept it, so wireless needs strong encryption to match."

4 marks: one factor at a time, both media in every sentence, with the reason.
